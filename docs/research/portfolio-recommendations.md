# Famous portfolios and recommendations for the ML Trading Platform

_Research run 2026-10-04. Six Sonnet researchers covered the literature, a seventh pulled 5 years of IBKR data, one Opus agent wrote the recommendations and a second Opus agent reviewed them adversarially. This is educational research for a personal project, not individual financial advice. Jurisdiction, account type (taxable or retirement) and horizon are unknown and would change parts of it._

> **How much to trust the numbers.** The proxy blocked portfoliocharts, bogleheads and most data sites, so the long-run figures in Appendix A come from search snippets. Treat anything not marked verified as unverified. The 5-year IBKR check (Oct 2021 – Sep 2026, total return, monthly rebalance, no costs or taxes) is real data, but it covers one regime and is far too short to rank portfolios. The reviewer independently verified these: Do & Faff pairs decay (0.86% → 0.37% → 0.24% per month); the Faber GTAA independent replication for 2006–2025 (6.05% CAGR, Sharpe 0.68, -11.7% max drawdown); SG Trend 2022 (+27.3%); and Doeswijk et al. (4.45% real return, 11.2% volatility, 3.39% excess return, so Sharpe ≈ 0.3, not 0.69).

## 1. Bottom line

1. **No famous portfolio dominates. They differ in how they fail.**
   - 60/40 and the retail All Weather fail when stocks and bonds fall together. In 2022, 60/40 lost 16.6% and All Weather lost 19.3%. All Weather's 40% in long Treasuries (TLT -31%) made it the worst of the group over the 5 years: 2.0% CAGR.
   - Gold and cash mixes (Permanent, Golden Butterfly) held up better in 2022 but lag in equity bull markets.
   - Trend rules (Faber GTAA, dual momentum) cut drawdowns but cost return in 2023–26 and have weakened since they were published.
2. **The evidence that your platform's current signals make money is weak.**
   - Daily technical patterns show essentially no edge once you correct for data-snooping and costs (Sullivan-Timmermann-White; Bajgrowicz-Scaillet).
   - Pairs trading returns have decayed about 70%, to near the cost of trading (Do-Faff).
   - A next-day-direction ML model on 39 hand-picked symbols does not inherit the results of large cross-sectional studies (Krauss et al.; Gu-Kelly-Xiu).
   - Only slow, diversified trend-following has a long, broad record, and what it buys is drawdown control rather than extra return.
3. **So put real money in a diversified, lightly tuned core, with at most a small rule-based trend sleeve.** The platform's own signals should get **0% real capital** until they pass pre-registered out-of-sample tests. Expect few or none to pass, and that is the honest outcome.
4. **The platform's most valuable job is as your allocation and risk cockpit.** That means a portfolio-level total-return backtester, an ETF universe, a monthly rebalance proposal that you approve and place in IBKR yourself, and a risk engine for paper trading. It stays paper-only, with no live mode.

## 2. My recommended design (after the reviewer's corrections)

The first Opus draft used 50% equity, 10% gold, 5% commodities, 10% TIPS and no long bonds. The reviewer showed that draft was built around 2022 hindsight:
- Its lead over 60/40 was mostly gold, which rose about 128% over the window.
- TIPS fell 12.1% in 2022, so they did not "survive" it.
- Dropping long bonds removes the deflation hedge.

I've adopted the reviewer's version: global market weights, tilted modestly.

### Layer 1: Strategic core, about 90% of investable capital

| Sleeve | Weight | Example ETFs |
|---|---|---|
| US total market | 32% | VTI / ITOT |
| Developed ex-US | 18% | VEA / IEFA |
| Emerging markets | 5% | VWO / IEMG |
| Intermediate Treasuries | 13% | IEF / VGIT |
| Long Treasuries (deflation hedge; yields about 5% now) | 7% | TLT / VGLT |
| TIPS (short-duration if the aim is inflation protection) | 7% | VTIP / SCHP |
| T-bills | 8% | SGOV / BIL |
| Gold | 5% | GLDM / IAUM |
| Broad commodities (optional insurance, near-zero expected premium) | 0–5% | PDBC |

- **Rebalancing:** check quarterly. Trade a sleeve when it drifts by more than the larger of 5 points absolute or 25% relative, with a minimum trade of 2 points. Use new contributions and dividends first.
- **No stops, take-profits or automatic drawdown halts on the core.** At -20% from peak, a review alert goes to you. It is not an automatic sell.
- **Crypto (optional):** a spot bitcoin ETF, capped by risk contribution at 4–5% of portfolio risk. That probably binds below 2% of notional. Treat it as speculation, not a hedge: it fell with stocks in 2022.

### Layer 2: Trend sleeve, 10% (priced insurance, not alpha)

- Faber-style rule on 5–7 ETFs (SPY, VEA, IEF, GLD, DBC, VNQ): hold each only when its month-end close is above its moving average, averaged across 8-, 10- and 12-month lookbacks; otherwise hold T-bills. Trade the next session, long-only, no leverage.
- In the 5-year data it **lowered** excess Sharpe and cost about 0.8 points a year, in exchange for a roughly 1.7-point smaller drawdown. Only keep it if you value that insurance.
- Hold it in a tax-advantaged account, because it turns over often.

### Layer 3: Platform signal satellite, 0% now and at most 5% later

The technical patterns, pairs and ML signals run in paper only. They graduate only if all of these hold:
1. The Phase 2 correct engine is in place.
2. They pass a pre-registered backtest test: a Benjamini-Hochberg or Holm correction across every hypothesis tested, plus a Deflated Sharpe p-value below 0.05 on the fixed calendar hold-out from the roadmap.
3. Paper trading matches the backtest within tolerance, with zero risk-engine breaches. Paper trading is an implementation check, not the statistical evidence.
4. Scale-up needs a probabilistic Sharpe ratio (PSR) of 95% or more on paper returns. Realistically that takes 18–24+ months.

Never use shorts, margin, leveraged or inverse ETFs.

### What the reviewer insisted I tell you
- I have **not** validated the core over a long history. Before trusting any drawdown expectation, run it on mutual-fund or index proxies back to 1990 or ideally 1972: VFINX/VTSMX, EAFE, VFITX/VUSTX, VIPSX, gold spot, a commodity index and FRED T-bills. Cover 2000–02, 2008, 2013, 2020, 2022 and the 1980–2000 disinflation.
- Expect years where this trails SPY by 3–6 points. The biggest risk is behavioural: abandoning the plan. Write a one-page Investment Policy Statement with targets, bands, the review schedule and a no-override rule.
- Tax location: trend, TIPS, commodities and REITs go in tax-advantaged accounts. Gold ETFs in a US taxable account are taxed as collectibles. Watch wash sales between similar sleeves. Outside the US, use UCITS equivalents.

These incorporate the reviewer's changes: heat capped at 4%, an earnings/event blackout, and an allocation bypass guard.

- An allocation-tagged order is accepted only if all of these hold:

  - the order moves the sleeve toward a versioned target;

## 5. Answers to the open roadmap questions

**Unattended paper orders vs research screen (roadmap Q1)**

Research screen by default. The core and trend sleeves produce a monthly rebalance proposal that the owner approves and places manually (IBKR). Unattended orders are allowed only in PAPER, only after the Phase 1 chokepoint plus a Phase 2 BH/Holm correction exist, and only for a small frozen set (5 or fewer) of pre-registered strategies. The purpose of that experiment is to measure paper-vs-backtest divergence and build an out-of-sample record, not to make money. Expect few or zero orderable signals after the fixes; accept that as the honest result. No live mode (keep paper=True hard-coded with a test).

**Which asset classes are tradable (Q2)**

Executable in paper: US-listed ETFs (the core and trend universe) and the US large-cap equities, long-only. Crypto: exposure only through spot ETFs in the core; the 8 crypto symbols stay research-only (no shorting, no brackets on Alpaca, weekend gaps). Forex and futures: research-only, used as trend-signal inputs. Drop the futures pairs GC/SI and especially CL/NG (no stable economic link) from the default scan. Add bond, TIPS, gold, commodity and T-bill ETFs, which the current universe lacks entirely.

**Shorts / margin (Q3)**

No shorts, no margin, no inverse or leveraged ETFs. A SELL signal means exit the long or hold cash, and the sell qty is capped at the held qty. Consequence: pairs trading cannot be executed and stays a research diagnostic. Its documented edge has decayed to near cost anyway (Do-Faff: 0.24%/month 2003-09 before borrow costs). For pairs research, MAX_POSITION_SIZE should mean gross exposure across both legs (Q5).

**Risk limits (Q4)**

See risk_defaults. In short, for the signal sleeve: 0.5% risk per trade with a 2xATR(20) stop, 10% per-symbol notional cap, 100% gross / 5% heat, at most 8 positions with at most 2 per cluster, at most 5 orders/day, -1.5% daily and -3% weekly entry halts, drawdown ladder 5/8/10% ending in a persisted manual-restart halt, max order = min(10% equity, $ cap). For the core: band rebalancing, no stops, review trigger at -20%.

**Scope trim**

Keep and fix: data layer (now with total-return/dividend-adjusted data and 15-20+ years of history), the backtester (next-bar entry, costs), the risk engine and chokepoint, stress tests (add named 2008/2020/2022 windows), and the React frontend. Add: portfolio backtester, ETF universe, trend sleeve, rebalance proposals. Freeze: the 20 patterns (keep 2-3 trend-type rules such as MA cross and Donchian breakout as benchmarks; mark oscillator and candlestick-style patterns research-only), the ML next-day classifier (no new models; if revisited, reframe as a cross-sectional ranker with purged walk-forward), and pairs (diagnostic only). Drop: live mode (WS1.6 live parts), intraday timeframes (Q6: daily bars only, monthly for allocation), per-symbol pattern tuning (Q12: global fixed rules), Kalman/triple-barrier/meta-labelling (WS5.4), the LLM orchestrator and eval harness (WS5.1 beyond a cheap summary), the Streamlit dashboard once React has parity (Q9), and the heavy Phase 4 stack (keep BH plus one Deflated Sharpe gate, PBO advisory). Also fix survivorship: the watchlist is hand-picked winners (NVDA, META, TSLA, SOL), so add broad-index controls and some laggards to any signal study.

**Reviewer corrections to these answers:**
- Add a `policy_allocation` eligibility class to the WS1.3 chokepoint instead of exempting allocation orders from validation.
- Amend the WS0.4 interlock so that allocation BUYs which reduce drift toward target are allowed.
- Use the fixed calendar hold-out date for every signal gate.

## 7. What not to do

- Do not add a live-trading mode or let the platform place orders in the real IBKR account; keep paper=True hard-coded and human-approve real rebalances.
- Do not copy the retail All Weather (40% long Treasuries): it is a duration bet that only looked safe in the 1981-2020 bond bull; it was the worst portfolio in the 5-year check (2.0% CAGR, -19% in 2022).
- Do not choose weights from the 5-year data check or from Golden Butterfly/BAA-style backtests; the window flatters gold and commodities and the Sharpe differences are statistical noise.
- Do not apply a 10% drawdown halt, 2% stops or 4% take-profits to a diversified allocation; they will force selling at the bottom of ordinary bad years.
- Do not size by 'confidence' derived from in-sample win_rate*profit_factor, and do not use full Kelly from backtested Sharpe; at most quarter Kelly from a haircut estimate, and in practice the fixed 0.5% risk rule.
- Do not enable shorts, margin, leverage, inverse/leveraged ETFs or direct crypto trading.
- Do not treat a positive pattern/ML/pairs backtest as an edge until it survives the multiple-testing correction, walk-forward, costs and a paper record; expect most to fail.
- Do not use VAA/DAA/BAA or single-asset dual momentum as the core; they carry the highest data-snooping and concentration risk.
- Do not treat bitcoin as a hedge or size it in dollar terms above about 2%; it becomes the dominant portfolio risk.
- Do not build all of Phases 3-5; cut scope to what serves allocation, risk and honest validation.
- Do not rely on any single research figure here without checking the primary source: most numbers came from search snippets because primary sites were egress-blocked.

The core-minus-60/40 monthly difference has t = 0.9. (2) The Sharpe ratios use a zero risk-free rate. ### Biases flagged

- Hindsight/2022 design bias: the core's distinctive sleeves (gold, commodities, cash, no long bonds) are exactly the 2022 survivors. A 5% commodity sleeve is justified almost entirely by DBC's +19% in 2022, though long-run broad commodity futures returns are near zero after roll (Erb-Harvey 2006; DBC had a negative return over 2008-2020).
- Recency/regime bias against duration: the report generalises 'no long Treasuries' from one rate shock. Long Treasuries were the best hedge in 2008 (TLT about +34%) and 2020 (about +18%). TLT's Sep-2026 price (77.78) is far below its 2022 low (96.11), so starting long yields are now much higher than in 2021. The forward case for some duration is stronger now, not weaker. Stock-bond correlation has fallen back to about 0.23 (rolling 12m).
- Gold momentum/valuation bias: a 10% gold weight is set after GLD rose 128% in 5 years to record highs. Gold has had multi-decade real drawdowns (about -80% real 1980-2001) and a long-run real return near zero. It also provides the core's whole in-sample edge over 60/40.
- Inflation-regime bias: the core is tilted for stagflation. In a 1980-2000-style disinflation, the 25-35% in gold, commodities, TIPS and cash would likely lag 60/40 by 1-3 pts/yr for decades, the kind of lag owners abandon.
- US home bias and EM omission: 70% of equity is US (35/50) vs roughly 60-64% of global float. EM gets 0% in the core even though VWO was fetched, and there are no non-US bonds. US equity valuations in 2026 are high relative to ex-US, which argues for at least market weight abroad. Quoting '~10% nominal long run' for SPY uses trailing returns, not valuation-conditioned expectations (most 10-year capital market assumptions are well below that).
- Start-date and window bias: 59 monthly observations with a start just before 2022, and the full-period SPY number is flattered by the 2023-26 run. The report acknowledges this, then still reports and uses ranks.
- Survivorship of famous portfolios: the reviewed set consists of portfolios that became famous because their backtests looked good, so comparing them inherits selection bias. The report applies this skepticism to Golden Butterfly and BAA but not to its own blend.
- Evidence-transfer bias for trend: the report borrows long/short managed-futures evidence (Hurst-Ooi-Pedersen; SG Trend 2022) for a long-only ETF timing sleeve. In this data the sleeve lowered excess Sharpe (0.68 to 0.64).
- Measurement bias: zero-rf Sharpe in a 4-5% cash-rate era, and SHY used as 'cash', both distort comparisons between low-vol and high-vol mixes.
- Investor-profile assumption: the report silently assumes a US-taxable individual with tax-advantaged space and USD liabilities. Jurisdiction, account type (individual, retirement or corporate), horizon and liabilities were never established.

### static-allocations
Static allocations differ mostly in how they fail. Every number below came from search snippets, because the egress proxy blocked portfoliocharts.com, bogleheads.org and advisorperspectives.com. I did not open any primary paper or fund document, and I have not checked any snippet against its source page. Many cells are therefore marked unverified, and every figure should be rechecked at the source before the platform relies on it. The main evidence: 60/40 lost about 17.5% in 2022, its worst year since 1937, and lost about 14.2% in 2008, a year when Treasuries helped. Permanent Portfolio and Golden Butterfly use gold and cash and had shallower 2022 losses, though the search results disagree on how shallow (-12% for the Permanent Portfolio, -14.4% max drawdown for Golden Butterfly). Faber's Ivy timing overlay claims a large drawdown cut (46% to under 10%, 1973-2008), but that is a backtest by the strategy's author and it is not a static allocation. The Doeswijk et al. global market portfolio (1960-2017) is the only peer-reviewed long-sample item, with a 0.69 Sharpe. Several of these portfolios (Golden Butterfly, All Weather retail ETF mix, Permanent Portfolio weights) are tuned to or popularized by historical results, so out-of-sample expectations should be lower than the backtests.

#### Classic 60/40 (US equities / US bonds)
- **Rule:** 60% US equities, 40% US intermediate/aggregate bonds. Common rebalancing is annual or 5% bands. These are conventional definitions, not verified against a primary document.
- **Stats:** 1928-2022: about 8.1% per year (snippet attributes this to a blog/asset manager; the exact source is unverified). 200-year long-run CAGR of about 7.3%, with stocks and bonds both down in only 16 of 200 calendar years (Eaton Vance blog snippet, https://eatonvance.com/insights/blog/200-years-of-data-affirm-our-long-term-confidence-in-the-6040-portfolio.html). Volatility, max drawdown and Sharpe: unverified.
- **Out of sample / 2022:** 2008: about -14.2% (stocks -37%, 10-year Treasuries +20.1%; search snippet, source page not opened). 2020: unverified. 2022: about -17.5%, worst since 1937 (https://www.fool.com/investing/2023/01/26/2022-was-the-worst-year-since-1937-for-this-invest; BofA via AOL: https://www.aol.com/news/60-40-strategy-worst-year-since-1936-114330825.html).
- **Strengths:** Simple and cheap. Strong long-sample record. Bonds cushioned 2008 and the bond-friendly decades.
- **Weaknesses:** The stock-bond correlation turned positive in 2022, and the portfolio had no inflation hedge. About 90% of its risk comes from equities. Worst year in the sample is probably the 1930s or 2022.
- **Sources:** https://eatonvance.com/insights/blog/200-years-of-data-affirm-our-long-term-confidence-in-the-6040-portfolio.html; https://www.fool.com/investing/2023/01/26/2022-was-the-worst-year-since-1937-for-this-invest; https://www.aol.com/news/60-40-strategy-worst-year-since-1936-114330825.html

#### Bogleheads three-fund portfolio
- **Rule:** Total US stock market, total international stock market and total US bond market. Weights are set by the investor's risk tolerance. A typical split is 40/20/40 (age-in-bonds style). Rebalance annually or when an asset class drifts 5 points or more. Not verified: bogleheads.org was blocked.
- **Stats:** Unverified. No sourced CAGR, volatility, drawdown or Sharpe figures. It behaves like a 60/40 variant with some international equity, so the 60/40 figures are a rough proxy only.

### risk-parity-vol-targeting
Research was constrained: WebFetch was egress-blocked for every primary host I tried (AQR, Bridgewater, SSRN, Wikipedia, Bogleheads, Lehigh, LBS, Alpha Architect), so I relied on WebSearch result summaries. Treat figures as secondary-sourced unless marked otherwise, and re-verify before building on them. Bottom line: (1) The economic idea behind risk parity (low-risk assets earn higher risk-adjusted returns, so overweight them) is well supported by long-sample evidence (Asness-Frazzini-Pedersen 1926-2010), but the full benefit needs leverage, and the 2022 stock-bond correlation flip showed the strategy is a bet on the stock/bond relationship. (2) Volatility-managed portfolios (Moreira-Muir 2017) look strong in-sample but are fragile out-of-sample according to Cederburg et al. (JFE 2020) and de Miguel et al. (JF 2024); expect little or no gain from vol-timing a single equity position. (3) HRP has weak support: the headline results are from the author's own Monte Carlo, with limited independent real-world evidence. (4) For an unlevered retail ETF version, the robust parts are inverse-vol or risk-budget weighting across a few diverse asset classes (equities, intermediate/long Treasuries, TIPS, gold, maybe commodities) with slow monthly rebalancing. Do not expect 'risk parity returns' without leverage, because unlevered risk parity is just a bond-heavy low-vol portfolio. All of this is subject to hindsight bias: the 1980-2020 falling-rate era flattered bond-heavy and levered-bond strategies, and the strategy names were popularized after strong results.

#### Bridgewater All Weather / classic risk parity
- **Rule:** Allocate by risk, not capital, across growth-up, growth-down, inflation-up and inflation-down environments (equities, nominal long bonds, inflation-linked bonds, commodities/gold), levered so the portfolio hits a target volatility (commonly ~10-12%). Retail proxy often quoted: 30% stocks, 40% long Treasuries, 15% intermediate Treasuries, ~7.5% gold, ~7.5% commodities (unlevered, so effectively a bond-heavy portfolio). Bridgewater primary page could not be fetched, so the exact institutional weights and leverage are unverified.
- **Stats:** AFP 2012 (1926-2010): unlevered risk parity stock/bond excess return 2.20% vs market portfolio 3.84%; levered to the market's 15% vol, risk parity excess return 7.99% (source below, via search summary of the CFA Institute abstract). RPAR ETF: roughly -32% drawdown from its Nov 2021 high through 2022 (secondary source, wealthmanagement.com snippet). Claimed Bridgewater All Weather 2022 drawdown ~22% with stock-bond correlation moving from ~-0.20 to ~+0.65: from a low-quality blog snippet, UNVERIFIED. Retail 'All Weather' ETF-style version: one 10-yr figure of 5.65% annual return, 5.81% vol, -9.9% max drawdown from an Italian site for an adapted version; period unspecified, treat as UNVERIFIED illustration.
- **Out of sample / 2022:** 2022 was the stress test: stocks (about -18%) and bonds (about -13%) fell together per a secondary summary (approximate, UNVERIFIED precise values); levered bond sleeves amplified losses. Columbia Adaptive Risk Allocation and Invesco Balanced-Risk Allocation reportedly slightly beat a traditional balanced portfolio in 2022 (Milliman FRM snippet, UNVERIFIED detail). Risk parity's 2008 relative success and 2010s popularity came during a four-decade bond bull market, so post-publication and hindsight effects are large.
- **Strengths:** Theoretically grounded (leverage aversion / betting-against-beta logic); diversifies across macro regimes rather than just equity beta; long-sample evidence of higher Sharpe than market-cap portfolio; avoids relying on forecasts of returns.
- **Weaknesses:** Needs leverage to deliver its edge (financing and margin risk); structurally long duration, so it is exposed when rates and inflation rise together (2022); relies on a stable or negative stock-bond correlation, which was positive in the 1970s-90s and again in 2022; backtests benefit from the 1981-2020 falling-yield era; unlevered versions lose most of the return advantage.

#### Volatility-managed portfolios (Moreira-Muir 2017) and its critiques
- **Rule:** Scale exposure to a factor/market by the inverse of its trailing realized variance (e.g., prior-month realized vol), holding the scaling constant c fixed so average vol matches the unmanaged portfolio. Moreira-Muir apply it to market, value, momentum, profitability, ROE, investment factors, and the currency carry trade; monthly reweighting.
- **Stats:** Moreira-Muir (JF 2017; data 1926-2015): reports large alphas and higher Sharpe ratios for vol-managed factors (qualitative via NBER/JF abstract; exact alpha figures, e.g. the commonly cited ~4-5% annual market alpha, UNVERIFIED because I could not fetch the paper). Cederburg, O'Doherty, Wang, Yan (JFE 2020): across 103 equity strategies, spanning-regression alphas are positive but real-time implementable versions generally earn LOWER certainty-equivalent returns and Sharpe ratios than unmanaged portfolios; the cited cause is structural instability in the spanning regressions. Exact share of strategies and Sharpe deltas UNVERIFIED.
- **Out of sample / 2022:** Cederburg et al. is itself the out-of-sample evidence: the in-sample alphas rely on look-ahead in the scaling constant and unstable regression coefficients. De Miguel, Martin-Utrera, Nogales, Uppal (JF 2024), 'A Multifactor Perspective on Volatility-Managed Portfolios', argues that the Moreira-Muir results are strongest for single factors and that a multifactor approach (managing each factor's vol while accounting for the others) is needed; details of out-of-sample verdict UNVERIFIED beyond the title and general framing. I did not obtain any verified 2022 number for vol-managed equity.
- **Strengths:** Intuitive and cheap to implement (one scalar per asset); reduces tail exposure from volatility clustering; the fact that realized vol is persistent while expected return is not is a plausible economic basis; useful as a risk-control layer even without alpha.
- **Weaknesses:** Alpha claims shrink or vanish with real-time constants and transaction costs; heavy turnover at monthly frequency; sensitive to sample (the 1930s and 1987 dominate); post-publication decay likely; works best at factor/long-short level, not obviously for retail long-only ETFs. Be skeptical of using vol targeting as a return enhancer, treat it as a risk dial.
- **Sources:** https://www.nber.org/papers/22208; https://alphaarchitect.com/does-portfolio-timing-based-on-volatility-signals-outperform-buy-and-hold/; https://www.lehigh.edu/~xuy219/research/COWY.pdf; https://lbsresearch.london.edu/id/eprint/3716

#### Hierarchical Risk Parity (Lopez de Prado 2016) and inverse-volatility weighting
- **Rule:** HRP: (1) cluster assets using a correlation-distance matrix (hierarchical tree), (2) quasi-diagonalize the covariance matrix, (3) recursively bisect and allocate inversely to cluster variance. Needs no covariance inversion, so it is stable when the matrix is ill-conditioned. Inverse-vol (IV): w_i proportional to 1/sigma_i, ignoring correlations. Both are long-only, no-leverage by construction.
- **Stats:** Lopez de Prado (SSRN 2708678, 2016): Monte Carlo experiments show HRP has lower out-of-sample variance than CLA minimum-variance, and per secondary summaries also vs inverse-variance and equal-weight; exact variance numbers UNVERIFIED. These are the author's simulations, not live results; I found no verified long-sample ETF backtest figures for HRP.
- **Out of sample / 2022:** No verified 2022 figures for HRP or inverse-vol multi-asset portfolios. By construction, an inverse-vol portfolio with a large bond share would have been hurt in 2022 (bonds were the low-vol assets that lost money), so expect drawdowns similar in sign to risk parity, smaller in size due to no leverage (reasoning, not a verified number).
- **Strengths:** Robust to estimation error; no leverage; very simple IV baseline is hard to beat once estimation noise is considered (a general finding in the covariance-shrinkage literature, cited from general knowledge, UNVERIFIED here); HRP gives sensible clusters for a heterogeneous universe like the owner's 39 symbols.
- **Weaknesses:** Evidence mainly from the proposer's simulations and later replications of mixed quality; post-publication performance claims are contested; clustering can be unstable across windows; correlation-blind IV overweights low-vol assets (bonds, forex) and can be concentrated in a few clusters.
- **Sources:** https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2708678; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hierarchical_Risk_Parity; https://quantpedia.com/hierarchical-risk-parity/; https://www.mathworks.com/help/finance/create-hierarchical-risk-parity-portfolio.html

Given Cederburg et al., prefer to treat any gain as unproven.
- Replace the placeholder 5% per-position / 15% total exposure with risk-based limits derived from the target portfolio vol (e.g., 8-10% annualized) and a per-sleeve risk-contribution cap; the 2% stop / 4% take-profit rules are incompatible with a slow rebalanced allocation and should be dropped for this sleeve.
- Include 2022, 2008, 1970s-style regimes (via long-history proxies or mutual-fund data) in a stress suite; require the allocation to survive a joint stock-bond drawdown scenario before any paper orders. Report results net of costs and with a lagged-signal (next-bar) convention, consistent with the audit's bias findings.
- Treat HRP as an optional research module for the existing 39-symbol universe and 10 pairs (to cluster correlated assets and avoid over-allocating to the crypto and tech clusters); benchmark it against inverse-vol and equal-weight, and accept the result if it is not clearly better. Apply multiple-testing discipline: this adds only a handful of hypotheses, unlike the ~780-hypothesis scans.
- Keep it a research screen / paper-only recommendation engine first (consistent with reviewer advice against live mode); the risk-parity layer outputs target weights and a rebalance list that a human approves, rather than unattended orders.

### trend-tactical
Trend and tactical rules show a real but modest, regime-dependent edge, and it is mostly risk reduction rather than extra return. Long-sample evidence (Hurst/Ooi/Pedersen, 1880-2016, 67 markets) supports time-series trend. Out-of-sample, the edge decayed: Faber GTAA Sharpe fell from 0.81 (1972-2005) to 0.68 (2006-Mar 2025, a third-party figure); GEM trailed buy-and-hold after about 2010; and the SG Trend index returned roughly 0.4-1.8% a year over 2009-2018 before +27% in 2022. Trend rules did cut drawdowns in 2008 and 2022. They were weak in 2023-2025 and drawn out in the 2010s (SG Trend, CTA index data). VAA/DAA/BAA are the weakest evidence: tuned on the same data, with little genuine post-publication record found. Cost and tax drag is large when turnover is high (monthly rebalancing, taxable accounts). Sourcing caveat: my fetch tool was egress-blocked for most primary sites (AQR, SSRN, Yale PDFs, Advisor Perspectives, CXO, Cambridge). Nearly every number below therefore comes from search-engine summaries of those sources, not from reading the primary document. Treat all numbers as 'secondary-source, verify before relying'. Items I could not find are marked unverified.

#### Faber GTAA-5 (10-month SMA, 2007 paper and 2018 update)
- **Rule:** Five asset classes at 20% each: US stocks, foreign stocks, US 10y bonds, REITs, commodities. At each month-end, an asset is held if its price is above its 10-month SMA (about the 200-day MA). Otherwise its 20% slice goes to T-bills. Monthly evaluation. Long-only, no leverage, no shorts.
- **Stats:** Original backtest 1972-2005: CAGR 11.7%, max drawdown 9.5%, Sharpe 0.81 (secondary summary; original paper unread). 1973-2012: timed 10.5% vs buy-and-hold 9.9% annualized; drawdown cut from about 46% to under 10% (etf.com/Faber summaries via search). 2006-Mar 2025 (third-party replication, not Faber): Sharpe 0.68, CAGR 6.05%, max DD 11.7%. A separate source says Faber's 2018 JPM update reported Sharpe 'from 0.45 to 0.80', but I could not confirm what that compares (possibly buy-and-hold vs timing); treat as unverified.
- **Out of sample / 2022:** Faber's own 2018 update (JPM) concludes the model 'performed well in real-time, achieving equity-like returns with bond-like volatility and drawdowns' (abstract via search). That is the author grading his own rule. An outside replication shows return decay (11.7% to about 6% CAGR) while the drawdown cap held (about 10-12%). It underperformed stocks in 6 of the 8 years after the 2008-09 bear market (Swedroe/etf.com summary). 2022: the stock-and-bond downturn hit it less than 60/40, but I have no verified 2022 number (unverified).
- **Strengths:** Simple, fully rule-based, daily or monthly data only. Few parameters, so little overfitting. Consistent drawdown control. Easy to run on ETFs (SPY, EFA, AGG/IEF, VNQ, DBC/GLD). The Sharpe edge came from lower volatility, not higher return.
- **Weaknesses:** Return lags buy-and-hold in long bull markets. Whipsaw around the MA, with high turnover and short-term gains in taxable accounts. Results are sensitive to rebalance day and to MA lookback (QuantConnect and Bogleheads threads note implementation variance). Hindsight bias: the asset list and 10-month rule were chosen after the 1970s-2000s sample, and the 2007 paper became the most-downloaded SSRN paper, which invites crowding. Post-publication decay is visible.
- **Sources:** https://www.etf.com/sections/index-investor-corner/swedroe-why-financial-trends-persist; https://concretumgroup.substack.com/p/global-tactical-asset-allocation; https://mebfaber.com/2013/06/08/qtaa-update-global-tactical-5/; https://www.bogleheads.org/forum/viewtopic.php?t=173272; https://www.quantconnect.com/forum/discussion/8285/a-quantitative-approach-to-tactical-asset-allocation-gtaa-5-with-simplemovingaverage/; https://publishedresearch.cambridgeassociates.com/revisiting-tactical-asset-allocation/

#### Antonacci Dual Momentum / GEM (Global Equities Momentum)
- **Rule:** Monthly. Compare US equities (S&P 500) vs ex-US equities (ACWI ex-US) by trailing 12-month return (relative momentum). If the winner's 12-month return also beats T-bills (absolute momentum), hold it. Otherwise hold aggregate bonds. 100% in one asset at a time.
- **Stats:** Original backtest 1974-2013: CAGR about 17.4%, max drawdown about -22%, Sharpe about 0.9 (secondary summary). 1971-2026 replication (SSRN, Petit): CAGR 15.18% vs S&P 500 11.27%, max DD 21.7% vs 50.9%, Sharpe 0.83 vs 0.50. 2016-2026: CAGR about 12.3%, max DD -17.8%, Sharpe 0.72 (portfoliodb-style summary, unverified quality). One source says it has trailed a passive index by about 4.8 points a year since 2010, with its worst drawdown in 55 years in 2021-2023. These come from search summaries, not primary reads.
- **Out of sample / 2022:** Antonacci's own extension (1950-2018) claims two out-of-sample windows (1950-1973 and 2012-2018) that were still attractive. Independent summaries say the post-2010 record lags the S&P 500 and has its deepest drawdown in 2021-2023. That is consistent with the 12-month signal being late at the 2022 equity-bond peak. Newfound/ReSolve notes that the COVID crash of March 2020 whipsawed it and that model-specification risk (the exact lookback, rebalance date, ETF choice) can swing annual returns by hundreds to thousands of bps. I recommend running several implementations.
- **Strengths:** Very low turnover (12-month lookback, a few switches a year). Clear rules. Big drawdown avoidance in 2000-02 and 2008. Cheap to implement with 3 ETFs.
- **Weaknesses:** Concentrated: one asset at a time, so high specification risk. Lags in V-shaped markets. Post-2010 US equity dominance penalized the rotation. Switches realize short-term gains (taxes). Only one clean post-2013 out-of-sample decade, and it is mediocre against buy-and-hold. Hindsight: the 12-month lookback, 3-asset universe and 1974 start date were fixed after seeing the data.
- **Sources:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7427878; https://investresolve.com/inc/uploads/pdf/global-equity-momentum-a-craftsmans-perspective.pdf; https://www.advisorperspectives.com/articles/2019/05/06/global-equity-momentum-a-craftsmans-perspective; https://www.optimalmomentum.com/extended-backtest-of-global-equities-momentum/; https://www.portfoliodb.com/portfolios/gem-dual-momentum; https://www.traderounds.com/p/model-autopsy-dual-momentum; https://blog.thinknewfound.com/category/portfolio-construction/page/6

#### Keller & Keuning VAA / DAA / BAA (canary-universe breadth momentum)
- **Rule:** VAA: use a fast momentum score (13612W, a weighted average of 1/3/6/12-month returns). If any asset in the offensive universe has negative momentum, go 100% to the best defensive asset. DAA (2018): replace that rule with a separate canary universe (VWO, BND) and use breadth (the share of canaries with negative momentum) to set the cash fraction. BAA (2022): slow relative momentum for the offensive pick plus fast absolute momentum on the canaries; BAA moves to the defensive universe if any canary is negative. Monthly.
- **Stats:** BAA: Dec 1970-Jun 2022 backtest, returns of 20% or more with monthly max drawdowns of 15% or less (CXO summary of the SSRN paper). BAA spends about 60% of the time defensive. DAA out-of-sample universe test: 1994-Mar 2018. I did not verify per-strategy CAGR, Sharpe, turnover or cost-adjusted numbers (unverified). 20%+ CAGR is far above any live-tracked trend or tactical strategy and should be read as in-sample optimism.
- **Out of sample / 2022:** Little verified post-publication data. Author-run out-of-sample checks (DAA tested 1994-2018) are partly contaminated because the lineage is the same authors tuning VAA to PAA to DAA to BAA on largely overlapping data. I found no independent live-tracking numbers (Allocate Smartly tracks them behind a paywall) and no verified 2022 or 2023 result: unverified.
- **Strengths:** Breadth-based canary signal cut the crash-protection lag of single-asset absolute momentum. Public papers, transparent rules, and an explicit out-of-sample attempt. Fits a monthly daily-bar pipeline with about 10-20 ETFs.
- **Weaknesses:** Highest data-snooping risk of the group: several generations of the same idea tuned on the same history, and the BAA headline of 20% CAGR with 15% drawdown is the kind of result that decays. High turnover (monthly all-in switches to bonds or cash, whole portfolio in 1-3 assets, mostly short-term tax treatment). Concentration. Sensitive to ETF proxies and the data start.
- **Sources:** https://www.cxoadvisory.com/strategic-allocation/multi-class-momentum-portfolio-with-canary-crash-protection/; https://thechartist.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/5369/SSRN-id4166845.pdf; https://www.quantconnect.com/forum/discussion/14842/hybrid-asset-allocation/; https://vvv.quantpedia.com/?p=25576; https://yagogarcia.substack.com/p/estrategia-baa-una-aproximacion-agresiva

#### Time-series momentum (Moskowitz, Ooi, Pedersen 2012) and a Century of Evidence (Hurst, Ooi, Pedersen 2017)
- **Rule:** TSMOM: for each liquid futures contract, go long if its trailing 12-month excess return is positive and short if negative, with positions scaled to a constant ex-ante volatility target (about 40% annualized per instrument in the paper), diversified across 58 contracts in equity indices, bonds, commodities and currencies. Century paper: a 1/3/12-month lookback blend with volatility scaling across 67 markets (29 commodities, 11 equity indices, 15 bonds, 12 currency pairs).
- **Stats:** MOP 2012: 58 futures, 1985-2009; positive risk-adjusted returns in every asset class; a diversified TSMOM portfolio had large abnormal returns with little factor exposure and did best in extreme markets (the 'smile'). Component gross Sharpe numbers from search summaries (e.g., 12-month TSMOM 1.17 for traditional assets, 1.34 for alternatives, 1.60 diversified across groups): unverified per-figure. Century paper 1880-2016: gross return 18.0% annualized, net of 2/20 fees 11.0%, net Sharpe about 0.76 (alternative summary says 0.4 average net Sharpe, which is probably a different cut: unverified, the sources disagree). Net returns were positive in every decade, the lowest being 4.1% (the decade starting 1919). Correlation to stocks and bonds was near zero.
- **Out of sample / 2022:** Post-2009 decay is documented: a commodity TSMOM replication shows strong pre-2009 and weaker later returns (search summary of an independent study). Managed futures in practice: SG Trend annualized about 0.4-1.8% over 2009-2018 vs a 5.7% full-history average, max drawdown 21.8% (secondary source, angelinvestorsnetwork/IBKR campus). 2022: SG CTA Index +20.1%, best since 2000 inception (Hedgeweek); SG Trend +27.3% (alpha-week/Hedgeweek). 2023: SG Trend -4.16% (toptradersunplugged). AQR and Cambridge Associates both published pieces on trend's recent struggle ('You Can't Always Trend When You Want'). Cambridge concludes that it is not structurally broken. Both are interested parties.
- **Strengths:** The strongest and longest evidence base: 137 years, 67 markets, positive in every decade. A long-short, multi-asset diversifier with crisis convexity (2008, 2022). Works in the 'bonds and stocks fall together' regime where 60/40 fails.
- **Weaknesses:** Needs futures, leverage and shorts. A retail platform would use ETF proxies, which weakens it. Long flat or negative stretches (about 2009-2019, 2023-2025). The century paper is by the same authors who run the strategies (AQR), and it is partly in-sample by construction. Fees and costs matter a lot (gross 18% vs net 11%). Instrument-level Sharpe numbers are inflated by in-sample choice of lookback.
- **Sources:** https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2089463; https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/A-Century-of-Evidence-on-Trend-Following-Investing?aqrPDF=1; https://fairmodel.econ.yale.edu/ec439/hurst.pdf; https://fairmodel.econ.yale.edu/ec439/jpde.pdf; https://www.cxoadvisory.com/momentum-investing/trend-following-with-intrinsic-momentum-over-the-very-long-run; https://alphaarchitect.com/trend-following-is-everywhere/; https://www.hedgeweek.com/trend-followers-turn-leaders-ctas-deliver-record-returns-2022/; https://www.toptradersunplugged.com/?p=14211; https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/White-Papers/You-Cant-Always-Trend-When-You-Want?aqrPDF=1; https://www.cambridgeassociates.com/insight/does-trend-followings-recent-struggle-signal-that-the-strategy-is-structurally-broken/; https://angelinvestorsnetwork.com/market-analysis/managed-futures-cta-funds-hedge-inflation-2026

#### Costs, whipsaw, turnover and tax effects (cross-cutting)
- **Rule:** Evidence on how implementation frictions change the results of the rules above: monthly signals, ETF or futures execution, and taxable vs tax-advantaged accounts.
- **Stats:** Century paper shows fees dominate: gross 18.0% to net 11.0% (1880-2016, 2/20 plus costs). Faber and GEM are low-turnover (monthly check, a handful of switches a year); VAA/DAA/BAA are high-turnover. I found no verified turnover percentages or bps-cost figures for any strategy (unverified). Tax: tactical strategies are tax-inefficient in taxable accounts, and short-term gains can offset the alpha (Allocate Smartly/Freefloat summaries; no verified tax-drag number). One analysis finds that after-tax tactical results can be close to pre-tax static results (a worst-case study, secondary).
- **Out of sample / 2022:** Whipsaw cases: the COVID crash of March 2020 (GEM, trend rules switched late); 2010-2019 flat CTA years; 2023. 2022 was the best case for trend, since both stocks and bonds fell and absolute-momentum filters moved to cash or short bonds. The Faber 2018 update also notes that cash-management choices (T-bills vs other) changed results, which means part of the returns come from the implementation, not the signal.
- **Strengths:** Monthly cadence and ETF execution make costs small relative to futures CTAs. Tax-advantaged accounts (IRA or similar) neutralize the biggest drag. Low turnover favors Faber and GEM over VAA/DAA/BAA.
- **Weaknesses:** Short-term gains taxed as ordinary income in taxable accounts. Frequent all-in switches (BAA) magnify slippage and model specification risk. Backtests of tactical models often omit costs, taxes and the exact execution day.
- **Sources:** https://allocatesmartly.com/tax-efficient-tactical-asset-allocation/; https://freefloat.substack.com/p/static-vs-tactical-allocation; https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/A-Century-of-Evidence-on-Trend-Following-Investing?aqrPDF=1; https://blog.thinknewfound.com/category/portfolio-construction/page/6

**Takeaways for the platform:**

- Add one portfolio-level trend allocator as the first thing built. The platform currently runs per-symbol backtests with no allocation layer. A monthly, long-only, equal-weight 10-month-SMA rule over ETFs already in or near the universe (SPY or IWM, a foreign equity ETF, a bond ETF, GLD or DBC, a REIT ETF) with T-bills as cash is the lowest-risk way to add allocation. It needs about 30 lines of logic, uses daily bars you already have, and has the best-documented drawdown behavior.
- Use the trend rules as the benchmark and risk overlay for the existing ML and pattern signals, not as another alpha scan. Test whether RF/XGB/LGBM and the 20 patterns beat a simple 10-month-SMA benchmark with the same costs. If they do not, that is a strong signal to trim scope.
- Do not expect the published headline numbers. Plan on a Sharpe around 0.5-0.7 and a max drawdown of 10-20% (Faber 2006-2025 replication, GEM 2016-2026), not the 20% CAGR claims of BAA or the 0.8-0.9 in-sample Sharpe figures.
- Treat VAA/DAA/BAA as low priority or research-only: highest turnover, the most data-snooping risk (tuned sequentially on overlapping data), and no verified post-publication record here.
- If you add GEM, run multiple parameterizations (lookback 6/9/12 months, different rebalance days) and average them, because model-specification risk is large.
- Make signal timing explicit for the correction of the same-bar-entry bias: signal on month-end close, trade on the next open or close, and include costs. Trend rules are especially sensitive to look-ahead because the signal is a moving average of the same prices you trade.
- Include post-publication splits in the backtest report: in-sample vs published date (Faber 2007, MOP 2012, Century 2013/2017, GEM 2012-2014) and the 2022 and 2023 windows, so decay is visible. Also report the 2009-2019 flat spell for trend.
- Trend works best as a diversifier. The 39-symbol universe is mostly equity-heavy (17 US stocks and ETFs) plus crypto, forex and futures. A true time-series momentum sleeve across the 7 futures, 7 forex and 8 crypto symbols is possible with ETF or futures proxies, but shorts and leverage are open owner decisions and I would not enable them for live trading first.
- Keep the risk limits in line with the evidence: Faber and GEM drawdowns of roughly 10-22% suggest the 10% drawdown halt would trigger often and would lock in whipsaw losses. Consider a halt of 15-20% for monthly-rebalanced allocations, tested in the backtest first, with the 2% stop and 4% take-profit placeholders removed from the trend sleeve (the rule already is the exit).
- Keep the allocator paper-only or signal-only (order proposals) until Phase 1 (safe order chokepoint) exists. Monthly cadence suits a research screen plus a manual or confirmed paper order, and avoids unattended orders. Use a tax-advantaged IBKR account if it is ever live, because the strategies generate short-term gains.

### signal-evidence
Evidence on this platform's signal families (technical rules, pairs, ML next-day direction), searched 2026-10-04 and read as a candid review. Verdict: the published edge in each family is small, decays after publication, and mostly disappears once you correct for data snooping and add realistic costs. A retail trader on daily bars should expect roughly zero alpha from the technical-rule scans. Pairs and ML next-day direction are marginal at best. The strongest long-sample evidence is for slow, diversified trend-following/time-series momentum, which is a risk-premium-like strategy rather than an alpha source. It is built on monthly or longer signals, not daily chart patterns. The platform's own ~780-hypotheses-per-scan design is exactly the setup Harvey-Liu-Zhu and Sullivan-Timmermann-White warn about. Caveats on method: WebFetch was blocked for ideas.repec, aqr.com, cxoadvisory and nber.org, so every number below comes from search-result snippets rather than from reading the papers. Anything not in a snippet is marked unverified.

#### Classic technical rules (RSI, MACD, MA crosses, breakouts)
- **Rule:** Brock-Lakonishok-LeBaron (1992) tested 26 simple rules (MA crosses, trading-range breakouts) on DJIA. Later work re-tests them with data-snooping controls: Sullivan-Timmermann-White 1999 (7,846 rules, ~100 years of DJIA daily data, White Reality Check), Park-Irwin 2007 survey, Bajgrowicz-Scaillet 2012 (DJIA daily 1897-2011, false discovery rate).
- **Stats:** Park-Irwin 2007: 58 of 92 'modern' studies found positive results for technical strategies, but profits were found 'at least until the early 1990s' and most studies suffer data snooping, ex post rule selection, and poor cost/risk estimation. STW 1999: after Reality Check, no significantly profitable rule for DJIA, S&P 500 index, or S&P 500 futures. Bajgrowicz-Scaillet: investors could never have picked the future best rules ex ante, and even in-sample the performance is offset by low transaction costs. No retail-realistic CAGR or Sharpe figure was verified (unverified).
- **Out of sample / 2022:** Out-of-sample is where these rules fail: STW 1999 extended the BLL rules to later data and to S&P 500 futures and found no significant profitability. No specific 2022 figure for these rules was verified (unverified).
- **Strengths:** Cheap, transparent, daily. Trend-type rules (MA cross, breakout) are the same idea as the long-sample trend-following evidence below, so a small number of them can have a real risk-premium basis. Useful as risk filters or regime context rather than as standalone alpha.
- **Weaknesses:** Many rules on few instruments invites false discoveries. Rule performance is not persistent. Costs erase in-sample profits. Oscillator-type patterns (RSI, candlestick-like patterns) have no comparable long-sample support in anything I found (unverified beyond the absence of evidence). Publication and hindsight bias: the rules that look good today were chosen after seeing the data.
- **Sources:** https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jecsur/v21y2007i4p786-826.html; https://ideas.repec.org:443/a/eee/jfinec/v106y2012i3p473-491.html; https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/119144; https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/361689

#### Pairs trading (distance and cointegration)
- **Rule:** Gatev-Goetzmann-Rouwenhorst (2006) distance method: formation period, trade the top pairs on divergence, close on convergence. Do-Faff (2010, 2012) extend the sample and add costs.
- **Stats:** GGR: up to ~11% annualized excess return for self-financing pair portfolios, US CRSP 1962-2002 (as summarized in search results; source below). Do-Faff, July 1962-June 2009: top-20-pairs mean excess return 0.86%/month in 1962-1988, 0.37% in 1989-2002, 0.24% in 2003-2009. After commissions, market impact and short fees the strategy remains profitable only at 'much more modest levels', with about 30 bp/month risk-adjusted for well-matched pairs within refined industry groups. Up to 70% of the profit drop was attributed to worsening arbitrage risk.
- **Out of sample / 2022:** The decline over time is itself the out-of-sample evidence: 0.86% to 0.24% per month across the three sub-periods. Both contrarian strategies Do-Faff examined are largely unprofitable after 2002. 2022 performance was not verified (unverified). The distance-method results are for hundreds of stocks, not 10 hand-picked pairs.
- **Strengths:** Market-neutral by construction, so it diversifies directional exposure. Economic rationale (relative mispricing), and a documented small positive net edge when pairs are industry-matched. Cointegration screening is more principled than raw distance matching (verification of its edge unverified).
- **Weaknesses:** Edge has shrunk about 70% since the early sample. Needs shorting and borrow, which conflicts with a long-only paper setup. Daily-bar cost drag is large. Selecting 10 pairs from a 39-symbol universe is a tiny sample, and pair selection on the same data used to backtest is exactly the multiple-testing problem. Cross-asset 'pairs' (SPY/QQQ, XOM/CVX) are cheaper to find but less diversified than the stock-pair universe in the papers.
- **Sources:** https://nber.org/papers/w7032; https://alphaarchitect.com/2013/06/pairs-trading-kicks-butt/; https://www.cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/pairs-trading-net-profitability/; https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2010/does-simple-pairs-trading-still-work; https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/41722/

#### ML next-day direction ensembles (RF/XGB/LGBM)
- **Rule:** Krauss-Do-Huck (2017): DNN, gradient-boosted trees and random forest, trained on lagged returns of all S&P 500 constituents (survivor-bias eliminated), ensemble long-short portfolio of top/bottom k stocks daily. Gu-Kelly-Xiu (2020): ML (trees, neural nets) on stock-level characteristics, monthly horizon, cross-section of US stocks.
- **Stats:** Krauss et al. 2017: ensemble out-of-sample returns above 0.45% per day for k=10 BEFORE transaction costs (as summarized in search results). The abstract, which I could not fetch, reportedly says profitability declines after the early-2010s and is weak after costs; I recall this but did not verify it (unverified). Gu-Kelly-Xiu: neural-net value-weighted long-short decile spread earns an annualized out-of-sample Sharpe of 1.35, and an S&P 500 timing strategy using NN forecasts earns Sharpe 0.77 vs 0.51 buy-and-hold (sample period not verified, unverified). Dominant predictive signals across all models: momentum, liquidity, volatility.
- **Out of sample / 2022:** Krauss: the pre-cost daily edge is large but the daily-turnover long-short design is cost-sensitive; post-2010 decay is unverified. GKX is a true recursive out-of-sample exercise at monthly frequency with large panels. Neither maps onto 17 US names plus a few crypto/forex/futures series with ~1 feature set. No 2022 figure was verified (unverified).
- **Strengths:** Nonlinear ML on large cross-sections with proper walk-forward validation shows real predictive gains in the academic setting. Ensembles reduce variance. Most useful as a cross-sectional ranker, not a next-day binary classifier.
- **Weaknesses:** Both papers use hundreds to thousands of stocks and long histories. Your 39 symbols give little cross-section, so the ML edge from those papers does not transfer. GKX gains are concentrated in small, illiquid stocks and are monthly, not daily (concentration claim from memory, unverified). Your audit already notes in-sample ML evaluation, which inflates accuracy. Next-day direction accuracy for liquid large caps is near 50-52% even in good studies (unverified), and costs eat that. Daily turnover is where costs hurt most.
- **Sources:** https://cris.fau.de/publications/208534681; https://ideas.repec.org:443/p/zbw/iwqwdp/032016.html; https://www.nber.org/papers/w25398.pdf; https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3159577

#### Post-publication decay and multiple testing (meta-evidence)
- **Rule:** McLean-Pontiff (2016): 97 published return predictors. Harvey-Liu-Zhu (2016): 316 factors and a multiple-testing t-ratio hurdle.
- **Stats:** McLean-Pontiff: portfolio returns are 26% lower out-of-sample (post-sample, pre-publication) and 58% lower post-publication; the implied publication-informed-trading effect is about 32 points (58 minus 26). Declines are larger for predictors with higher in-sample returns, and returns are higher for high idiosyncratic-risk, low-liquidity stocks. Harvey-Liu-Zhu: 316 factors catalogued, and a new factor should clear a t-ratio above 3.0 rather than 2.0, with the authors arguing most claimed findings in financial economics are likely false.
- **Out of sample / 2022:** The McLean-Pontiff 26% and 58% are the out-of-sample and post-publication measures. A later paper (arXiv 2206.15365) argues most claimed cross-sectional findings are in fact likely true, so the 'most are false' conclusion is contested. I did not read it, so treat its details as unverified.
- **Strengths:** Gives a concrete haircut rule: assume at least 50% of any published backtest edge is gone, and demand t > 3 when you tested many variants.
- **Weaknesses:** These studies cover cross-sectional stock anomalies, not daily chart patterns on indices and crypto, so the numbers are an analogy, not a measurement for this platform. Your scans test about 780 hypotheses per run, so at t > 2 you would expect a large number of false positives by chance alone.
- **Sources:** https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/economics/2016-mclean.pdf; https://ivey.uwo.ca/media/3775549/pontiff.pdf; https://nber.org/papers/w20592; https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/taming-the-factor-zoo/; https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.15365

#### Time-series momentum / diversified trend-following (best-evidenced family)
- **Rule:** Hurst-Ooi-Pedersen (AQR): long/short trend rules over 67 markets (29 commodities, 11 equity indices, 15 bonds, 12 currency pairs), 1880-2016, monthly-scale signals, volatility-scaled.
- **Stats:** Gross return 18.0% annualized, net of costs and 2/20 fees 11.0%, with annual volatility 9.7% and a net Sharpe of 0.76 (1880-2016 as reported in search summaries; a different summary quotes about 0.4 average Sharpe after fees in a sub-measure, so the exact figure depends on the specification and I could not reconcile it). Net returns were positive in every decade, the lowest at 4.1% for the decade beginning 1919.
- **Out of sample / 2022:** The study is built on extending the sample backward to 1880, which is the best defence against data mining here. I did not verify its 2022 performance (unverified, though I recall trend-following did well that year). Note the post-2008 decade was weak for managed futures (from memory, unverified). These figures are for a 67-market, volatility-targeted portfolio, not for a 7-futures retail subset.
- **Strengths:** Long out-of-sample history, low turnover (monthly), works across asset classes, and diversifies equity risk, especially in crises. Your futures, forex and crypto symbols fit this family far better than daily pattern signals.
- **Weaknesses:** Returns are lumpy with multi-year drawdowns. The paper's Sharpe is for a diversified portfolio of 67 markets, so a concentrated 39-symbol universe will see a lower Sharpe and bigger drawdowns. AQR is a manager with an interest in the strategy, and backward extension of data (1880 futures proxies) has data-quality issues.
- **Sources:** https://www.TrendFollowing.com/whitepaper/Century_Evidence_Trend_Following.pdf; https://oxfordstrat.com/coasdfASD32/uploads/2016/03/A-Century-of-Evidence-on-Trend-Following-Investing.pdf; https://alphaarchitect.com/trend-following-is-everywhere/

**Takeaways for the platform:**

This is a rule of thumb from stock-anomaly studies, not a measurement for your universe.
- Best-evidenced family for this universe is slow trend-following/time-series momentum across your futures, forex, crypto and index ETFs, using monthly or weekly signals, vol scaling and few parameters. Treat it as a risk-premium sleeve, not as a source of alpha, and expect drawdowns of multiple years.
- Pairs: keep as a research/diagnostic tool only. It needs shorts, the documented edge has decayed to near cost level, and 10 hand-selected pairs chosen on the same data is a selection-bias setup. If kept, require out-of-sample cointegration stability and costed results.

### risk-sizing
Caveat on evidence: WebFetch was blocked by the egress proxy for most primary hosts (Duke, Berkeley, qoppac, Wikipedia, davidhbailey.com). Everything below rests on WebSearch snippets, not full-text reads. Numbers I could not confirm from a snippet are marked "unverified" and come from my background knowledge. The practitioner consensus is consistent across sources. (1) Size by risk, not by notional: Turtle-style 1% of equity per unit with stop at 2N, or Carver-style volatility targeting. (2) Never use full Kelly. Carver says use half the Kelly risk target, and Kelly vol target equals the Sharpe ratio under i.i.d. Gaussian returns. Estimation error in the Sharpe/edge makes even half Kelly aggressive for an unvalidated strategy, so a quarter of an honestly haircut Sharpe is the sane starting point. (3) Cap aggregate risk in layers: per-position, per-correlated-cluster, per-portfolio. The Turtles used 4 units per market, 6 per closely correlated group and about 12 total (a secondary web source, not the original rules). (4) De-risk on drawdown as a continuous scale-down (Grossman-Zhou / CPPI logic) with a hard halt that needs manual restart. (5) Do not scale up until live or paper results are out-of-sample and long enough to beat the multiple-testing problem. For this platform (about 780 hypotheses per scan, biased backtests), the realistic prior is that expected Sharpe is near zero, so the right default is small, capped, paper-only sizing. Recommended defaults are at the end under takeaways. They are my own synthesis. Parameter choices (for example 12% vol target, 7% heat) are judgment calls, not sourced figures.

#### Turtle rules: fixed-fractional unit sizing (1% of equity per unit, 2N stop)
- **Rule:** Unit = 1% of account equity / (N x dollar value per point), where N = 20-day ATR (Wilder smoothing). Stop at 2N from entry, so one unit risks about 1% (loss is capped at 1% only if the stop fills). Limits per web summary: max 4 units per market, 6 units across closely correlated markets, about 12 units across the whole portfolio. The 4/6/12 limits come from a secondary MQL5 article, not the original Faith/Curtis Faith rules; unverified against the primary text.
- **Stats:** Not a backtest, so no CAGR or Sharpe is given. Turtle program ran 1983-1988 (background knowledge, unverified in this session). Worst-case if all 12 units stop out together is about 12% x 2N-stop = 12% of equity in a gap-free world. Gaps make it worse.
- **Out of sample / 2022:** Unverified. Widely reported that trend-following CTAs (a related style) did well in 2022, but I did not verify any number here. The Turtle edge itself (Donchian breakouts) is a famous example of post-publication decay, and the rules were public from about 1996 onward, so do not assume the original returns persist.
- **Strengths:** Simple, auditable, risk is explicit per trade. Volatility-normalised: same dollar risk in quiet and wild markets. Explicit correlation-cluster and total caps. Works on daily bars, which matches this platform.
- **Weaknesses:** Stop-based risk assumes the stop fills at its level (gaps, crypto weekends, forex news breach it). 1% per unit with 12 units is aggressive for an individual with unvalidated signals. Cluster limits are crude (static buckets, not measured correlation). Hindsight: the Turtle record is a survivorship story from a small group.
- **Sources:** https://www.mql5.com/en/articles/23448; https://it.tradingview.com/script/OAVDLYGG

#### Carver 'Systematic Trading': volatility target + half-Kelly risk target
- **Rule:** Pick one annualised volatility target for the whole account. Carver's example is 25% (the web summary says he targets 25% average, with an instrument diversification multiplier, IDM, used to hit it across a portfolio). Position size per instrument = (cash vol target x forecast scaling x IDM x instrument weight) / instrument cash volatility, using recent daily return vol (he annualises daily vol by x16, about sqrt(256)). Kelly rule: optimal risk target equals expected Sharpe ratio (SR 0.5 means 50% vol). Carver recommends half of that, so 25% for SR 0.5. His blog also discusses 0.226 SR giving 22.6% full-Kelly vol, an illustration of the formula, not a recommendation.
- **Stats:** No performance stats in the sources I could read. Kelly arithmetic from the Carver summaries: full-Kelly vol = SR; half-Kelly vol = SR/2. With a realistic post-haircut SR of 0.3, half-Kelly is 15% vol and quarter-Kelly is 7.5%. These are my calculations, not Carver's numbers.
- **Out of sample / 2022:** Unverified. Carver publishes live futures results for his own account, but I could not retrieve them. Note his 25% target assumes a diversified futures book with a believed Sharpe near 0.5 or higher. That is a much stronger prior than this platform has.
- **Strengths:** One risk dial for the whole portfolio. Consistent across asset classes, so it fits a mixed universe of stocks, crypto, forex and futures. It explains how to derive the dial from the Sharpe you believe in, which forces honesty about expected Sharpe. Directly replaces the placeholder '5% per position' with a volatility-aware rule.
- **Weaknesses:** The target is only as good as the Sharpe estimate, and the Sharpe estimate from this platform's biased backtests is inflated. Realised vol is a lagging estimate and fails at regime breaks. The 25% target is high for a retail account with no validated edge. Needs a covariance or IDM estimate that is itself noisy with 39 symbols.
- **Sources:** https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/a-few-notes-on-systematic-trading; https://qoppac.blogspot.com/2020/03/how-much-risk-should-we-take.html; https://qoppac.blogspot.com/2022/06/vol-targeting-cagr-race.html; https://harriman-house.com/systematic-trading

#### Fractional Kelly (MacLean-Thorp-Ziemba) and estimation error
- **Rule:** Full Kelly maximises long-run log growth: f* = W - (1-W)/R for a simple bet, or mu/sigma^2 for a continuous asset. Fractional Kelly invests fraction f of the Kelly portfolio and 1-f in cash. Common choices are 1/2 or 1/4. Half Kelly is described as keeping about 75% of full-Kelly growth with about 25% of the variance (a widely repeated approximation from a secondary page, not read in the primary paper).
- **Stats:** Secondary sources: with perfect inputs, full Kelly has about a 50% chance of the account halving at some point (idealised model). Expected max drawdown roughly 50-80% of account (luxalgo, unverified quality). If true win rate is 55% but estimated at 60%, full Kelly overbets by about 50% (secondary source). None of these are from the primary paper text, so treat as indicative.
- **Out of sample / 2022:** Not applicable (a theory, not a strategy). Thorp's own use (blackjack, Princeton-Newport) is a survivorship story: I did not verify returns here.
- **Strengths:** Gives a principled upper bound on bet size. Asymmetry is the key lesson: overbetting beyond about 2x Kelly drives growth to zero or negative, while underbetting is mildly costly, so errors should go toward smaller sizes. Pairs naturally with vol targeting (Carver).
- **Weaknesses:** Requires knowing edge and variance. In practice both are estimated with large error, especially edge, so estimated Kelly is biased upward by selection (picking the best of about 780 hypotheses). Assumes i.i.d. returns and a known distribution. Fat tails make true Kelly lower. For a strategy whose edge is not yet proven, the right Kelly fraction may be effectively zero.
- **Sources:** https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~aldous/157/Papers/Good_Bad_Kelly.pdf; https://him-application.uni-bonn.de/uploads/media/DavisLleoKellyChapter.pdf; https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/kelly-criterion/; https://qoppac.blogspot.com/2020/03/how-much-risk-should-we-take.html

#### Volatility-managed exposure (Harvey, Hoyle, Korgaonkar, Rattray, Sargaison, Van Hemert, 'The Impact of Volatility Targeting', JPM Fall 2018)
- **Rule:** Scale exposure inversely to trailing realised volatility (standard deviation of daily returns) to hit a constant volatility target. The paper used a 10% target across 60 assets (equity, bond, credit, currency, commodity), daily data starting as early as 1926 and ending 2017.
- **Stats:** Qualitative from the abstract: vol targeting improves Sharpe for risk assets (equity, credit), explained by the leverage effect. Negligible Sharpe impact for bonds, currencies and commodities. Reduces the probability of extreme returns, with left tail less severe because crashes occur in high-vol regimes. Specific Sharpe numbers were not retrievable (Duke PDF blocked).
- **Out of sample / 2022:** The paper ends in 2017 so 2022 is out of sample, and I did not verify 2022 results. Known caveat from background knowledge (unverified here): vol targeting can lag in fast V-shaped reversals and was hurt in March 2020 rebound and in 2022 when stocks and bonds fell together. Benefits to Sharpe have been reported to be weaker for post-publication periods; unverified.
- **Strengths:** Long sample (1926-2017). Multi-asset. Mostly a tail and drawdown benefit, which is exactly what a small retail account needs. Cheap to implement with daily bars.
- **Weaknesses:** Sharpe gain is concentrated in equities and credit, so for this universe the gain applies to the 17 stock/ETF symbols but not much to forex or futures. Authors are from a trend-following CTA (AHL), a possible conflict of interest. Lag in vol estimation. Transaction costs from rebalancing, which the paper's frictionless numbers may understate.
- **Sources:** https://alphaarchitect.com/volatility-targeting-improves-risk-adjusted-returns/; https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1370354; https://www.cxoadvisory.com/equity-premium/benefits-of-volatility-targeting-across-asset-classes; https://people.duke.edu/~charvey/Research/Published_Papers/P135_The_impact_of.pdf

#### Drawdown-based de-risking (Grossman-Zhou 1993 / CPPI) with halt-and-restart
- **Rule:** Grossman-Zhou: for CRRA investors who want drawdown never to exceed a fixed fraction of the running peak, optimal risky exposure is proportional to the 'surplus' W_t - alpha*M_t (wealth minus the allowed fraction of the running maximum). CPPI is the same idea: risky allocation = multiplier x (wealth - floor), the rest in cash. Practical version: exposure scale = max(0, (equity - floor)/(peak - floor)) or a stepwise cut at set drawdown levels. A hard halt needs a defined restart rule. See the arXiv drawdown-with-restart paper for a data-driven approach.
- **Stats:** No numeric results retrieved. These are theory papers; numbers on drawdown limits are chosen by the user (the 'alpha'), not estimated.
- **Out of sample / 2022:** Not tested here. Known downside from theory (and background knowledge, unverified): CPPI can 'cash out' at the bottom and miss the rebound (gap risk and whipsaw), and the Lund paper title notes the Grossman-Zhou strategy is 'not always optimal'.
- **Strengths:** Makes risk reduction mechanical and pre-committed, removing emotional decisions. Continuous scaling avoids the cliff of a single halt. Matches the platform's existing '10% drawdown halt' concept, but smoother.
- **Weaknesses:** Locks in losses after a drawdown (path-dependent). If the strategy has no edge, de-risking just slows the bleed, it does not fix it. Needs a restart rule or the account stays off forever. Theory assumes continuous trading and no gaps.
- **Sources:** https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/mathfi/v3y1993i3p241-276.html; https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.02613; https://lup.lub.lu.se/record/223851; https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/CPPI

#### Minimum track record and multiple-testing discipline (Bailey, Borwein, Lopez de Prado, Zhu 2014; Deflated Sharpe Ratio)
- **Rule:** The more configurations you try, the longer the backtest (or out-of-sample record) needed for a given Sharpe to be believable. Minimum Backtest Length (MinBTL) formalises this. The Deflated Sharpe Ratio adjusts the observed Sharpe for the number of trials, non-normality and track length.
- **Stats:** From the search snippet: after trying only 7 strategy configurations, a researcher is expected to find at least one 2-year backtest with annualised Sharpe above 1 when the true out-of-sample Sharpe is 0. The '45 trials needs 5 years' figure I recalled could not be confirmed in the snippet: unverified. This platform's roughly 780 hypotheses per scan is far above 7.
- **Out of sample / 2022:** Applies to any period. The paper is a statistical argument, not a market study.
- **Strengths:** Directly relevant: the audit found about 780 hypotheses per scan with no multiple-testing correction. It gives a quantitative reason to demand a long out-of-sample record before scaling.
- **Weaknesses:** MinBTL assumes independent trials, which overstates the penalty when the 780 hypotheses are correlated (many patterns on correlated symbols). Does not tell you a specific paper-trading duration without a Sharpe and trial count input.
- **Sources:** https://scholarworks.wmich.edu/math_pubs/40/; https://www.advisorperspectives.com/articles/2013/10/22/how-many-monkeys-does-it-take-to-find-a-successful-strategy; https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2460551

**Takeaways for the platform:**

- Replace flat '5% per position' with risk-based sizing: size = (risk budget per trade) / (stop distance in dollars), where stop distance = 2 x ATR(20) (Turtle) rather than a fixed 2% stop. A fixed 2% stop on daily bars is inside normal daily noise for crypto and high-beta stocks like TSLA/NVDA/AMD, so it will mostly measure noise (reasoning, not a sourced number).
- Default risk per trade: 0.5% of equity (range 0.25% to 1.0%). Justification: Turtles used 1% with an edge believed to be strong. This platform's edge is unproven and its backtests are biased, so start at half or less of that. Judgment call, not a sourced figure.
- Keep a hard per-position notional cap of 10% of equity (the Phase 1 chokepoint should enforce it), so a tight stop cannot create an oversized position. With 0.5% risk and a 2N stop of about 4% to 6%, notional is about 8% to 12% of equity, so the cap is about right. The existing 5% cap is acceptable for a first paper phase and is conservative.
- Portfolio heat (sum of open-risk-to-stop across positions): cap at 5% of equity at start (equals 10 positions at 0.5%). Turtle analogue is about 12 units x 1% = 12%, which I would not copy for an unproven system. Raise toward 8% only after the scale-up gates below are met. Unverified as a standard; my synthesis.
- Gross exposure: cap at 100% of equity (no leverage) in the first phase, versus the current 15% total exposure placeholder, which is so low that results will be dominated by cash. Alternatively keep 15% to 30% for the very first paper weeks to test the plumbing. Net cap by direction separately; start long-only for equities unless the owner decides on shorts.
- Max concurrent positions: 8 to 10, with at most 2 per correlated cluster (US index ETFs SPY/QQQ/IWM/DIA are effectively one cluster; mega-cap tech AAPL/MSFT/NVDA/AMZN/META/GOOG is another; energy XOM/CVX; banks JPM/GS/BAC; crypto as one cluster). Turtle analogue: 4 per market, 6 per correlated group (secondary source). Use measured 60-day return correlation as an additional check (reject if corr > 0.7 with an open position in same direction; the 0.7 threshold is a judgment call, unverified).
- Volatility target for the whole book once portfolio-level allocation exists: start at 8% to 10% annualised, not Carver's 25%. Derivation: Kelly vol = Sharpe. If you honestly haircut expected Sharpe to 0.3 (given the audit findings), half-Kelly = 15% and quarter-Kelly = 7.5%. Pick about 10% initially. Raise only with validated out-of-sample evidence. Harvey et al. used a 10% target in their study, which gives a supporting reference point.
- Fractional Kelly: treat Kelly only as a ceiling, never a target. Use at most quarter Kelly computed from a shrunk (haircut) edge estimate, and take the minimum of the Kelly size, the fixed-fractional size, and the notional cap. Because of selection bias from about 780 hypotheses, the edge for any 'winning' pattern should be shrunk heavily (or set to zero until it survives out-of-sample).
- Daily loss limit: halt new entries for the rest of the day at -1.5% equity (about 3 full-risk stop-outs at 0.5%). Weekly: halt at -3%. These are judgment calls, unverified as industry standards. Prop firms commonly use daily loss limits of about 3% to 5% (background knowledge, unverified).
- Drawdown de-risking ladder, replacing the single 10% halt: scale risk per trade to 50% at -5% from the equity peak, to 25% at -8%, hard halt with manual restart review at -10% (keeps the existing 10% halt as the final backstop). Continuous variant: exposure multiplier = (equity - floor)/(peak - floor) with floor = 0.90 x peak (Grossman-Zhou / CPPI form). Warn that this locks in losses and cannot rescue a strategy with no edge.
- Gate to scale up, not a time-only rule. Require ALL of: (a) at least 6 months and at least 100 closed trades of unattended paper trading with realistic fills (next-bar open, costs, slippage), (b) paper Sharpe shrunk by a deflated-Sharpe style correction for the number of strategies tried still above 0, (c) realised max drawdown within the modelled budget (not more than 1.5x the backtest expectation), (d) zero risk-limit breaches by the order chokepoint. Reference: Bailey et al. say 7 trials can produce a 2-year Sharpe above 1 by luck, so short windows prove little. The 6 months / 100 trades numbers are my judgment, unverified as a standard.
- Scale-up steps after the gate: double risk per trade only once per quarter, and only if the preceding quarter met the gates. Never above 1% per trade or 8% heat without a year of live (small real-money) results. Consider tiny live size on IBKR only after paper results; reviewer already recommended no live-trading mode, so keep this a human-gated decision.
- Implement all of this in the Phase 1 safe-order chokepoint as a single risk engine: per-trade risk, notional cap, heat, cluster count, daily/weekly loss, drawdown ladder, max order, kill switch. Log every rejection with the reason. Unit-test the limits (Phase 0). The $1000 max order can stay as an absolute backstop during paper.
